Research papers are intimidating. They are also, for the most part,
*written to be intimidating* — not deliberately, but because they're
optimized for an audience of six specialists, not for you.

Here's the reading method that finally made them stick.

## Three passes

**First pass (10 minutes).** Read the title, abstract, and conclusion.
Skim the figures. Decide: does this paper deserve my time? Most don't.

**Second pass (45 minutes).** Read the introduction and related work.
Read the method section once, without trying to understand the math.
Your goal is to finish with a one-sentence description of what the
authors did.

**Third pass (as long as it takes).** Re-derive the key equations.
Work through the experiments. Ask what you'd do differently. This is
the pass where you actually *learn*.

## What to write down

I keep a `notes.md` file for every paper I finish. It has four sections:

1. **The claim** — what does the paper argue, in plain words?
2. **The evidence** — what experiments or proofs support the claim?
3. **The gaps** — where is the paper weak, and what would I need to
   believe for the claim to hold?
4. **The reuse** — is there a technique, dataset, or framing I can
   take into my own work?

After a year of this, I have a searchable knowledge base. More
importantly, I've trained myself to read actively rather than decoratively.

## Don't read alone

The single biggest unlock: read with someone else. A friend, a study
group, a Discord server. Confusion shared is confusion halved. Most
papers I "understand" on my own, I actually only understood when I
tried to explain them.

Find your people. Start small. Keep going.
