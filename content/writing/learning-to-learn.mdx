There's a quiet trap in computer science education: the feeling that you're
always behind. A new framework launches. A research paper drops. Someone
online builds, in a weekend, something you thought would take you a year.

The honest answer is that none of this matters as much as it seems to.

## The scaffolding problem

Most of what I learned in my first two years wasn't about code at all. It
was about **scaffolding** — the habits and mental models that let me pick
up a new thing and actually hold onto it.

A few examples:

- Writing things down in my own words before I move on.
- Building the smallest possible working version first.
- Learning to sit with confusion without immediately googling my way out.

These sound like clichés. They are. But they're the kind of clichés that
only start to mean something once you've ignored them long enough to feel
the cost.

## Taste as a forcing function

Lately I've been thinking about *taste* — the slow-growing sense of what
is good, what is worth your time, what is merely impressive. Taste isn't
the same as skill. You can be a skilled programmer with terrible taste,
and a beginner with surprisingly good taste. The latter is better company.

Taste comes from reading a lot of code you didn't write, and asking: *why
did they do it this way?* Then: *what would I have done?* Then: *which of
us is actually right?*

## What I'm trying to remember

Computer science is a long game. The people who stay curious — about old
papers, new tools, the boring middle layer of the stack — are the ones
still doing interesting work a decade in. I want to be one of them.

More to come.
